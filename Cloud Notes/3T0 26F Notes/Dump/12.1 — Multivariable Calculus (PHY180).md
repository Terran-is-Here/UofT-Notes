Before starting off with this note, please retake the time to remember what the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is: 
$$
\begin{align}
\int f(x)  dx &= F(x)+C \\
\int F'(x)dx &= F(x)+C \\
\int _{a}^b f(x) dx&= F(b)-F(a) \\
\int_{a}^bF'(x)dx &=F(b)-F(a)
\end{align}
$$



## Multivariable Differentiation w/ One Variable
Recall that for a variable with only one derivative; it's corresponding derivative is simply taking it with respects to solely one variable: 
$$
f(x) \to f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx}
$$
Recall that we define this as the rate of change of some function $f(x)$ with respects to some term $x$.

In the case of a multivariable function, such as $f(x,y)\mapsto z$, we define the operation of a *partial* derivative; that is we take the derivative with respects to *one* of our variables, whilst holding the others constant. 
$$
\begin{align}
f(x,y) &=x^2y^3 \\
\frac{{\partial f}}{\partial y} & = \frac{x^2y^3}{\partial y} = 3x^2y^2 & \text{Power rule w/ respect to } y  \\
\frac{{\partial f}}{\partial x} & = \frac{x^2y^3}{\partial x} = 2xy^3& \text{Power rule w/ respect to } x
\end{align}
$$
Note that we considered our $x$ term to be some constant in terms of our differentiation rules. 

## Multivariable Integration with One Variable 
Recall that for a function with only one variable; calculating it's indefinite integral is akin to calculating it's antiderivative, plus some constant term at the end: 
$$
\int f(x) = F(x) + c
$$
For a multivariable function with an integral taken with respect to one of it's terms, we follow the same logic with holding the other variables within some $f$ constant, and taking the integral with the corresponding rules with the variable we are integrating with respects to. 

However, our $c$ term now instead becomes some function of the remaining variables that we failed to consider: 
$$
\int f(x,y) \ \partial x = F(x,y) + c(y)
$$
Where this can be evaluated to find the corresponding term; note that taking the definite integral: 
$$
\int_{A}^Bf(x,y) \ \partial x = F(B,y) - F(A,y) 
$$
