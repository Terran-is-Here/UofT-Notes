# Lecture Notes
## Arguments 
Recall from[[1 — The Praxis Design Framework, PRA01 Debrief#Black-Box Model / "Dam" Model| the Dam Model]] that engineers partake in arguments whenever they try to design things: 
* The nature of reality itself is often disputed. 
* Our models make an argument to what we end up perceiving. 
* Choices in designs are also an argument.
* Getting building permissions to construct designs also include an argument. 
* The *acceptance* of work within reality also requires an argument.
Arguments are omnipresent in all of the segments of Engineering Design. Making decisions and recommendations require arguments: 
* Decisions require justification, 
* Recommendations require proof. 

> [!theorem] Definition of an Argument (from MONTY PYTHON??)
> An argument is generally defined as a connected series of statements to establish a definite proposition: 
> > Connected as in integrated; of multiple parts, convincing, and focused.

## Toulmin's Structure of Argument
Source: Textbook pp. 19-24

Generally: 
* We start from a starting point of information (the "ground"). 
* From the information that we have; we try to make a claim (something we "assert" from the "ground") to convince someone to accept our *claim*. 
  These essentially loop the three steps below to further develop our claim. 
	* From a claim; we try to find evidence (data) and justification (logical reasoning) to support our claim. 
	* We may also require a qualifier (or constraint) to help support our claim. 
	* We may also consider potential *counterclaims* that may arise; that is other views that may contrast our current point. 

The Toulmin Structure *can* also be considered a method of developing arguments. Note 

> [!theorem] Ground
> The ground forms the fundamental backing for which we make our arguments; these form the context of the eventual claims that we form. 
> > Note however that the *available information* varies per individual; consider what information comes from a personal source and what originates from a globally available dataset. 
> > 
> > For instance: Information COVID-19 may be well known, but it may not be well known to someone isolated; thus not letting them share the same understanding of the ground that you do. 
> > 
> > Always consider what grounds are available for your contexts, and work from there. 

> [!theorem] Claims
> The Claim is something that we *will try to assert* to someone; that is this is the thing that we are arguing for acceptance to whomever is the recipient. These come in various types, in order of distance away from the Ground/backing knowledge. 
> > Analytical Claims: 
> > Answers "What" based off an available dataset / ground to generate a question to generate understanding. 
> 
> > Interpretive Claims: 
> > Answers 'So What?', important to generate agreement (i.e. what is the point). 
> 
> > Speculative Claim 
> > Answers "what could be?", important to generate any potential ideas. 
> > **This is normally where design work starts**
> 
> Note that the further we move away from the Ground, the more evidence and justification we need to do. 

> [!theorem] Evidence & Justification 
> These are logical / data-based figures which can *support* your claim. **At minimum; for an argument, you must have Evidence, Justification and a Claim.**

> [!theorem] Counterpoint
> These are potential counterpoints to your argument that you should consider when making your claim.
